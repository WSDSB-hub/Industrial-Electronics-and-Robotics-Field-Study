# Industrial Sensor Signal Chain and Anti-Interference Design: From Desktop Encoder EMI to Welding-Strength Electromagnetic Fields

## Background

In Project A (STM32 Motor PID Closed-Loop Control), I encountered a problem where encoder readings spiked to 14,000 RPM at high motor speeds. The root cause was that the encoder signal lines floated between pulses and were susceptible to electromagnetic interference generated by the motor, producing spurious edges. The solution was implemented in two layers: at the hardware level, enabling internal pull-up resistors on PA6, PA7, PB6, and PB7; at the software level, adding an outlier rejection filter. This was my first experience handling an EMI problem, but the solution was relatively rudimentary — the pull-up resistors merely pulled the floating state to a defined level but did not truly isolate the interference source.

During my internship at Tianyi Welding, I systematically investigated sensor selection and anti-interference design on the welding shop floor. The electromagnetic interference intensity in a welding environment far exceeds that of a desktop motor — hundreds of amperes of current variation, high-frequency radiation from IGBT inversion, and inrush surges at arc ignition — making the design of the sensor signal chain critical to system stability. By comparing industrial anti-interference solutions with my own desktop project, I gained a completely new understanding of sensor signal processing and electromagnetic compatibility design.

## 1. Typical Sensors in the Welding Environment and Their Signal Chain Design

The automated production lines at Tianyi Welding and the Panasonic GP5 welding machines use a variety of sensors. Each sensor's signal type and anti-interference measures are specifically designed for the strong electromagnetic interference environment of welding.

### 1.1 Output Current Hall Sensor (Built into the Welding Machine)

The Panasonic YD-350GP5 has a built-in L03S400D15WM Hall-effect current sensor for real-time detection of the welding output current. For internal signal transmission, a 0–5V voltage output is used. While a voltage signal is feasible for short distances inside the machine, an external metering instrument uses a shunt resistor with a 60mV proportional voltage. This is a typical industrial design trade-off: voltage can be used for short internal distances to save cost, while a low-impedance shunt signal is used for longer external connections to reduce interference coupling.

The signal cable is a shielded twisted pair, routed with physical separation from the main power path. This design philosophy is consistent with my approach when handling encoder signal interference — keeping signal lines as far away from interference sources as possible. However, in an industrial setting, this isolation is structural (zoned cabling, metal partitions) rather than merely trying to maximize distance on a small breadboard.

### 1.2 Coolant Flow Switch (Built into the Welding Machine)

The flow switch, model TSMS0003, outputs a passive dry contact (digital on/off). When the coolant flow is insufficient, the dry contact opens, triggering an Err-011 alarm and shutting down the machine.

A dry contact is a purely mechanical switching signal that requires no power supply, is immune to electromagnetic interference, and offers extremely high reliability. Its signal cable uses 2-core RVVP shielded control cable, routed along the machine chassis and kept away from the high-frequency inverter area. For safety-critical detection points in industrial settings (such as coolant flow, door interlocks, and emergency stop buttons), dry contacts are the most reliable signaling method.

In contrast, in my desktop project, I directly connected encoder pulses to STM32 GPIO pins without any electrical isolation. When the TB6612 suffered an internal breakdown, 12V high voltage backfed through the signal lines and destroyed the STM32 main chip. If optocoupler isolation had been added to the encoder signal lines, that burnout could have been prevented.

### 1.3 Shielding Gas Mass Flow Meter (Standard Equipment at Tianyi Welding Workstations)

The MF5700 series flow meter outputs a standard 4–20mA current loop signal. The signal cable is a 2-core shielded twisted pair (overall shield), routed separately along the workstation cable tray, with a horizontal spacing of ≥30cm from the welding power cables. At crossing points, a metal shielding sleeve is added.

### 1.4 Laser Seam Tracking Displacement Sensor (Standard for Laser Welding and Special-Purpose Machines)

The Keyence IL-100 series or domestic CRNT series laser displacement sensor is the core sensor for seam tracking. Its primary ranging measurement uses a 4–20mA current loop output; high-end models additionally incorporate an RS-485 or CANopen digital bus for parameter configuration. The sensor cable is routed entirely through metal drag chains in a dedicated control cable tray. The probe head is fitted with a metal light-shielding and anti-spatter cover. The cable maintains a horizontal spacing of ≥30cm from the welding torch power line and the main welding cable; long parallel runs are prohibited. At crossing points, cables must intersect at 90° and be wrapped with metal shielding foil.

### 1.5 Industrial Vision Positioning Sensor (For Vision-Guided Welding and Seam Identification)

Hikrobot industrial area-scan cameras or Keyence CV series vision systems transmit primary positioning data via Gigabit Ethernet / GigE Vision. Trigger and status signals are NPN/PNP digital switching signals and require optocoupler isolation. Some simplified models support 4–20mA output for seam offset values. The camera cable is routed separately through a metal flexible conduit, arranged in a different layer from the welding power cables, with a horizontal spacing of ≥30cm. The camera body is fitted with an anti-arc and anti-spatter metal shield. Cable connectors are sealed against water and spatter, and ferrite beads are added to both ends of the network cable for filtering.

## 2. 4–20mA Current Loop vs. 0–10V Voltage Signal: The Core Design Principle for Industrial Anti-Interference

In the strong electromagnetic interference environment of welding, the 4–20mA current loop is the dominant signal standard for analog sensors. Its four major advantages over the 0–10V voltage signal are outlined below. Understanding these represents the most important cognitive upgrade I gained from transitioning from board-level development to an industrial field setting.

### 2.1 No Attenuation Over Long-Distance Transmission

When a voltage signal is transmitted, the resistance of the wire itself creates a voltage divider effect. The longer the wire and the thinner its gauge, the greater the voltage error at the receiving end. A 4–20mA signal is a constant-current output; as long as the total loop resistance is within the transmitter's load capacity, the line resistance does not alter the current. The distance from a welding workstation sensor to the control cabinet typically ranges from several meters to over ten meters; the error of a voltage signal would be significantly amplified over such distances, whereas a current signal is inherently immune to attenuation caused by line resistance.

### 2.2 Inherent Immunity to Electromagnetic Interference

A voltage signal presents a high-impedance input, making it easy for ambient electromagnetic interference to couple into the line and cause value fluctuations. A current signal is a low-impedance loop, making it difficult for ambient electromagnetic interference to induce a significant current change. In the strong EMI environment of arc ignition and high-frequency IGBT inversion, the anti-interference capability of a current signal far exceeds that of a voltage signal. This is why nearly all analog sensors on the welding shop floor use the 4–20mA current loop rather than the simpler 0–10V voltage signal.

### 2.3 Broken-Wire Fault Detectability

By industry convention, 4mA corresponds to the zero point and 20mA to the full scale. If the line breaks, the current drops to 0mA, and the system can directly determine that a sensor fault or line break has occurred and trigger an alarm. In contrast, a broken 0–10V signal also reads 0V, making it impossible to distinguish between a "true zero" and a "line fault." In a welding automation scenario, the inability to detect a broken wire could allow the equipment to continue operating with a failed sensor, potentially leading to a serious accident.

### 2.4 Strong Compatibility with Industrial Standards

For PLC and motion controller analog input modules, 4–20mA is the standard interface. The automated special-purpose machine system for the rock drilling rig at Tianyi Welding uniformly uses 4–20mA interfaces for all analog sensors, compatible with the analog input modules of Siemens or Mitsubishi PLCs. A standardized signal standard reduces signal conversion steps and potential failure points.

### 2.5 Comparison with Desktop 3.3V GPIO Encoder Reading

The encoder signal transmission method I used in Project A was as follows: the encoder directly output a 3.3V pulse signal, connected via DuPont wires to the STM32 GPIO pins, and counted in the timer's encoder mode. This approach has three fundamental limitations.

**Transmission distance**: The wire length from the encoder to the STM32 did not exceed 20cm, suitable for short-distance transmission within a single device. Once the signal needs to cross devices or workstations, the attenuation and interference of a voltage signal become unacceptable.

**Anti-interference capability**: The 3.3V single-ended voltage signal had no anti-interference design whatsoever. When the motor was running, the encoder signal lines floated between pulses and were highly susceptible to electromagnetic interference from the motor, producing spurious edges — this was the root cause of the 14,000 RPM anomalous readings I observed.

**Electrical isolation**: The encoder signal was directly connected to the STM32 GPIO pins without any electrical isolation. When the TB6612 suffered an internal breakdown, the 12V high voltage backfed through the signal lines into the STM32, destroying the main chip. If optocoupler isolation had been present on the encoder signal lines, that burnout could have been prevented.

In contrast, the sensor signal chain design in an industrial environment follows fundamentally different principles: using current loops instead of voltage signals for long distances; using shielded twisted-pair cables with strictly enforced routing clearances in high-noise environments; adding optocoupler isolation on critical signal paths; and powering sensors from isolated power supplies. These design measures are not optional enhancements — they are the foundation that enables industrial equipment to operate stably under conditions of strong interference, long distances, and high reliability requirements.

## 3. Signal and Power Cable Routing Standards

The engineering construction standards at Tianyi Welding specify clear requirements for the routing of signal and power cables. These standards are a direct embodiment of electromagnetic compatibility design in engineering practice.

### 3.1 Clearance Requirements

For horizontal runs, the parallel spacing between analog/digital signal cables and 380V power lines or high-current welding output cables must be ≥30cm. A distance of ≥50cm is recommended between high-voltage power circuits and low-voltage signal cables. For the dedicated cables of laser and vision sensors, the parallel spacing from the main welding cables is more stringent, requiring ≥50cm.

### 3.2 Crossing Requirements

Where crossing is unavoidable, cables must intersect at 90° to minimize the electromagnetic coupling area. The crossing point must be wrapped with metal shielding foil or passed through a metal sleeve.

### 3.3 Shielding and Conduit

All analog signal cables must use shielded twisted-pair cable. The shield must be grounded at a single point (grounded at the control cabinet side, floating at the sensor side) to prevent a ground potential difference between the two locations from creating a ground loop. On-site cabling must be run through metal flexible conduit or galvanized steel pipe. Co-routing with power cables in the same conduit is prohibited. The metal conduit must be reliably grounded along its entire length to form a continuous shielding cavity. The cables for laser and vision sensors must be run through metal conduit or metal drag chains along their entire length; exposed cabling is prohibited.

These standards are conceptually similar to my approach of "keeping DuPont wires as far from the motor as possible" when dealing with encoder signal interference. However, the industrial on-site construction standards are systematic, quantitative, and reusable, whereas my solution was merely a temporary, experience-based workaround. This is the difference between "engineering" and "debugging."

## 4. Troubleshooting and Resolution of Sensor False Triggering Caused by Arc Welding Interference

The field engineers at Tianyi Welding have developed a systematic method for interference troubleshooting. When I asked whether they had encountered sensor false triggering due to arc welding interference, they provided a structured troubleshooting procedure.

### 4.1 Typical Symptoms

At the moments of arc ignition and extinction, sensor values jump, digital signals false-trigger, and analog signals fluctuate significantly. Encoder counts miss pulses and position deviations occur. Laser tracking exhibits deviation and vision systems misidentify objects. The equipment experiences irregular false shutdowns, with poor reproducibility of alarm signals.

### 4.2 Troubleshooting Steps

Step 1 — Distinguish the type of interference: Replace the field sensor with a standard signal source. If the value stabilizes, the problem is external line or radiated interference; if it remains unstable, the problem is power supply or conducted interference internal to the equipment. For vision and laser sensors, first block the arc light to determine whether the interference is optical or electromagnetic.

Step 2 — Investigate the grounding system: Check whether the shield is grounded at multiple points, whether a ground potential difference exists between the welding machine and the control cabinet, and whether the welding equipment has an independent ground connection.

Step 3 — Investigate the cabling: Check whether signal and power cables run in parallel over long distances, whether they are routed in the same conduit, and whether the shield is damaged.

Step 4 — Investigate the power supply: Check whether the sensor power supply shares a circuit with the welding machine and whether the power supply ripple exceeds specifications.

### 4.3 Resolution Measures

**Physical isolation**: Increase the distance between signal and power lines; route them in separate conduits. Add a metal light-shielding cover to the laser sensor probe, and add an anti-arc filter to the vision sensor.

**Optimize shielding and grounding**: Ground the analog signal cable shield at a single point. For high-frequency scenarios, implement equipotential bonding and then ground at both ends. Build an equipotential grounding grid for the control cabinet and provide a separate grounding electrode for the welding machine.

**Signal filtering**: Add RC low-pass filtering in hardware and moving-average or median filtering in software. Add optocoupler isolation and relay isolation to digital switching signals. Add DC-DC isolation modules to the power input of vision and laser sensors.

**Power isolation**: Power sensors from an isolated power supply; add power line filters and surge suppressors to block conducted interference paths.

**Upgrade the signal standard**: When voltage signal interference cannot be resolved, replace the sensor with a 4–20mA current loop type or upgrade to an RS-485/CAN digital bus to fundamentally improve anti-interference capability.

**Welding machine-side optimization**: Adjust the inverter frequency of the welding machine to avoid the sensor sampling frequency. Add ferrite beads to the welding machine output to reduce the intensity of high-frequency radiation.

**Laser/vision specific optimization**: For laser sensors, adjust the sampling filter level, trading response speed for stability. For vision systems, configure multi-frame confirmation and ROI region limiting to prevent arc light and spatter from interfering with recognition results.

### 4.4 Comparison with My Approach to Encoder EMI

The solution I used in Project A to address anomalous encoder readings — pull-up resistors plus software filtering — corresponds, within the spectrum of industrial field solutions, to only the most basic "signal filtering" level. When facing the same type of problem, the industrial approach advances simultaneously on five fronts: physical isolation, shielding and grounding, signal filtering, power isolation, and signal standard upgrading, until the problem is resolved. This multi-layered defense design philosophy is one of the most important cognitive gains from my internship.

## 5. Cognitive Upgrade: From Desktop to the Industrial Field

In Project A, my experience in handling sensor signal interference was discrete, reactive, and dependent on personal empirical judgment. In the industrial field, I observed a complete, systematic approach to sensor signal chain design — from signal standard selection (4–20mA vs. 0–10V), to cable selection (shielded twisted pair vs. ordinary wire), to routing standards (30cm clearance, 90° crossing, layered cabling), to electrical isolation (optocoupler, dry contact), to fault diagnosis methodology (the four-step troubleshooting procedure) — every link has clearly defined design criteria and engineering rationale.

This cognitive leap from "personal experience" to "systematic methodology" is something no desktop experiment could have provided. It allowed me to understand why industrial equipment can operate stably over the long term in harsh environments, and it showed me how much more I need to learn on my path in embedded system design.

## 6. A Concrete Improvement I Can Bring Back to My Own Project

If I were to redesign the circuit for Project B, I would add an optocoupler isolator (such as the 6N137 high-speed optocoupler) to the encoder signal lines. This would not alter the signal quality of the encoder but would ensure that if the TB6612 fails, the 12V high voltage cannot backfeed through the signal lines and destroy the STM32. This is the "proactive protection" mindset I learned from the industrial field — not remedying a failure after it occurs, but cutting off every possible fault propagation path at the design stage.
